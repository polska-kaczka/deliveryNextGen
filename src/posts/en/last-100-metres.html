When a business works out the cost of delivery, it usually looks at kilometres. Yet the most expensive stretch often barely shows on the map: the last hundred metres between where the courier stops and the customer’s door.

Where the minutes go

  • Parking. In dense streets, finding a spot can take longer than the drive itself.
  • Pedestrian zones and narrow lanes. The van stops at the edge of the old town and the rest is done on foot.
  • Gates and intercoms. No code, nobody answering, reception closed after hours.
  • Time windows. Customers are home in the evening; courier shifts end earlier.

Each of these is a few minutes on its own. Together they decide whether a delivery pays off and whether the food arrives hot.

What a pavement robot changes

A robot doesn’t look for parking, doesn’t end its shift and goes where pedestrians go. Orders reach it automatically, the customer follows it live, and the compartment opens with a one-time PIN — so nobody has to be at the door at an exact minute, they just walk out to the robot.

When a robot meets a situation it can’t solve on its own, an operator in our operations centre takes remote control within seconds.

When a robot isn’t the right tool

To be fair: a delivery robot is built for short, dense routes. It won’t replace bulky-item transport or long suburban runs. It works best where there are many orders over short distances — city centres, campuses and estates.
